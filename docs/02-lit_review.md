# Literature review for AITU Smart Schedule

Project: Development of a Web Platform for Planning Academic Streams with a Module for Selecting Courses, Instructors, and Booking Classrooms (working name: AITU Smart Schedule). Team: Aizada Sembayeva, Aiya Zhakupova. Supervisor: Nadirov Nurtas.

## 1. Introduction

Research on university course timetabling has standard formulations, public benchmarks and solvers that reach or approach proven optima. Much less work covers what an institution needs once it uses such a system: explaining why a timetable cannot be built, letting students enroll in sections without overbooking or unfairness, and checking whether the people who use generated timetables accept them. The web systems described in applied venues mostly lack an algorithm or an evaluation. This review argues that AITU Smart Schedule is justified by an integration gap. Each of its techniques (a constraint satisfaction model, a feasibility-first search followed by simulated annealing, minimal conflict sets, database-enforced booking) has support in the literature, but no source in the reviewed set combines them in one evaluated web system for a single university.

The review serves the first objective of the project proposal. Section 2.1 identifies the main academic constraints, and the design decisions in Section 2.7 are the candidate functional requirements that the literature supports. The review covers four themes that follow from the system's functions: the course timetabling problem and how it is solved and benchmarked; the explanation of infeasibility in constraint problems; the allocation of students to course streams (sections); and timetabling information systems. The core set consists of sixteen distinct papers published between 2021 and 2026 from the project's article collection (the `docs/articles` folder and the project files). Two files in the collection, "Design and Implementation of Web-based Smart .pdf" and "IJEME-V12-N2-5.pdf", are byte-identical copies of the same paper (Roy et al., 2022) and are counted once. A paper was included if it addresses university course timetabling, explanation of unsatisfiable constraint problems, course or section allocation, or timetabling software, and if it appeared in 2021-2026, following the project's target that most of the literature come from the last five years. Fifteen of the sixteen were read in full; for two long papers the appendices were only skimmed. For Harrabi et al. (2024) only the publisher abstract was available, so claims about it go no further than the abstract.

Older works are included where the project uses something they define directly: the complexity result usually cited for timetabling (Even et al., 1976), simulated annealing (Kirkpatrick et al., 1983), QuickXplain (Junker, 2004), the ITC-2007 competition and its Track 3 specification (Di Gaspero et al., 2007; McCollum et al., 2010), the UD2 benchmark formulation (Bonutti et al., 2012), and the origins of the UniTime solver (Müller, 2005, 2009). Official documentation is cited for tools the system depends on (OR-Tools CP-SAT, PostgreSQL) and for the infeasibility handling of two existing engines (UniTime, FET). Each source was appraised with the questions from Lecture 4.1: whether methods are transparent and consistent, whether results can be verified, and whether the authors acknowledge their own position. Weak venues are named as such.

## 2. Thematic critical review

### 2.1 Problem definition and constraint taxonomy

The reviews agree on the basic definition. Chen et al. (2021) describe university course timetabling as allocating courses to time slots and venues, where a feasible timetable satisfies all hard constraints and its quality is measured by violations of soft constraints. Abdipoor et al. (2023) treat it as a special case of graph colouring with R^E possible allocations of E events to R rooms, and Bashab et al. (2023) frame it as an NP-hard combinatorial optimisation problem in which every soft-constraint violation adds a penalty to the cost. All three restate NP-hardness without proving it. The classical source for the complexity of timetabling is Even et al. (1976). Its volume and pages are only partly verified, and its exact theorem should be checked in the abstract before it is quoted.

For AITU, practical hardness depends mainly on how tight the resources are. Rappos et al. (2022, p. 400) found that hardness on ITC-2019 instances was "only partially related to the size" and that "in some cases, in spite of the small number of classes, it was challenging to obtain even one feasible solution due to the high occupancy of rooms and time slots". Müller et al. (2025, p. 259) make the matching observation from practice that "fewer available times make for easier timetabling and better timetables". The project's planned experiments at 60, 80 and 95 per cent room load test this variable directly, and on this evidence they may show more about the algorithm than the planned scaling from 50 to 400 streams.

The constraint catalogues differ in granularity. Chen et al. (2021) list fifteen typical constraints (C1-C15), from lecturer clashes to "no last slot of the day". Abdipoor et al. (2023) separate nine hard (HC1-HC9) and seven soft (SC1-SC7) constraints and map which ones each benchmark uses; ITC-2007 Track 3 uses HC4, HC7, HC8 and HC9 together with SC4-SC7. ITC-2019 goes furthest, with nineteen distribution-constraint types such as SameStart, NotOverlap, MinGap and MaxDayLoad, each of which can be declared hard or soft (Müller et al., 2025; Rappos et al., 2022). Four of these types (MaxDays, MaxDayLoad, MaxBreaks, MaxBlock) cannot be decomposed into pairwise rules, and Rappos et al. had to enforce them with lazy-constraint callbacks.

The reviews also acknowledge that the hard or soft status of a constraint varies between institutions. Chen et al. (2021, p. 106522) state that "Constraints (hard and soft) are highly variable according to institutions. The same constraint may be hard/soft for one institution but soft/hard for the others." Ceschia et al. (2023, p. 1) go further: "every single institution has its own rules, conventions, and fixations, thus making each specific problem almost unique." Room capacity is an example. It is a soft constraint in ITC-2007 Track 3, penalised per excess student (Ceschia et al., 2026; Di Gaspero et al., 2007), while the room-allocation model of Antunes-Batista et al. (2026) treats insufficient capacity as hard. A system for one university therefore needs a configurable constraint catalogue in which the hard or soft status of a rule is data. The AITU Smart Schedule technical specification (hard H1-H4: room, teacher, stream and cohort clashes; soft S1-S5: teacher preferences, cohort gaps, uneven daily load, cross-course stream overlaps, empty seats) maps onto the standard vocabulary: H1 is RoomOccupancy, H2-H4 are variants of Conflicts, S2 corresponds to curriculum compactness, S3 to MinWorkingDays or MaxDayLoad, and S5 to the capacity-waste criterion of Antunes-Batista et al. (2026). Using that vocabulary keeps the thesis comparable with published work.

The quality of these reviews varies. Abdipoor et al. (2023) is the only one with a documented search (four filtering stages, 151 to 45 papers), although its quality filter favoured "more established journals" and the authors' own group's methods feature among those it rates highly. Chen et al. (2021) is a narrative survey of 35 papers with no stated search protocol. Bashab et al. (2023) is the weakest: it omits ITC-2019 although it was submitted in mid-2022, gives exact methods almost no treatment, describes simulated annealing without the acceptance criterion, and its Table 1 assigns 5% and 4.5% to two categories with the same count of seven papers. It is cited here for definitions only.

### 2.2 Benchmarks: ITC-2007 Track 3 and ITC-2019

The second International Timetabling Competition ran from August 2007 to January 2008 with three tracks, aiming to test techniques on realistic models and to bring research and practice closer (McCollum et al., 2010). Track 3, curriculum-based course timetabling, has four hard constraints (all lectures scheduled, room occupancy, curriculum and teacher conflicts, teacher availability) and four weighted soft constraints: room capacity (1 per excess student), minimum working days (5 per missing day), curriculum compactness (2 per isolated lecture) and room stability (1 per extra room) (Di Gaspero et al., 2007). Bonutti et al. (2012) later placed this formulation, named UD2, in a family of curriculum-based formulations with public validators, instances, reference scores and lower bounds, all built from real university data.

Ceschia et al. (2023) give the most detailed account of benchmark practice in the set. They organise educational timetabling around six standard formulations and report that the 21 "comp" instances from Udine are normally used without comp11, which is too easy, and comp15, which is identical to comp03 under UD2. They also report that nearly all comp instances are now solved to proven optimality, citing Bagger et al. (2019a). Several of their observations apply to application projects: data is often not public, formulations are often under-specified, code is rarely released, and specific case studies are rarely complemented by standard benchmarks. They recommend that authors of application papers identify "an underlying standard problem that could be a simplified version of their own specific one" and report results on it (Ceschia et al., 2023, p. 2). AITU Smart Schedule plans to follow that advice with a `.ctt` adapter that runs its engine on Track 3.

ITC-2007 did not fix the time limit as a number of seconds. Participants ran an organiser-supplied benchmarking program, which reported how long a solver could run per instance on that machine; the organisers' own run gave "approximately 300 - 500 seconds on a modern PC under Windows XP", and only single-processor runs were allowed (ITC-2007 benchmarking page, n.d.; Di Gaspero et al., 2007, Rule 10). Ceschia et al. (2023) accordingly report results under "the timeout fixed for the ITC-2007 dataset (300-500 seconds depending on the CPU)". Ceschia et al. (2026) calibrated the limit with the official tool on their Intel i7-7700 and obtained 192 s per run; the figure is specific to that machine. The thesis should run the benchmark program on its own test machine and report the value it gives. Because the rule assumes one processor while CP-SAT runs a multi-worker portfolio by default, a fair comparison needs CP-SAT limited to a single worker, or both settings reported.

The sources disagree about which methods lead on Track 3. Chen et al. (2021) rate a network-flow GRASP with simulated annealing above the ATS approach and credit an IP relaxation with improved lower bounds. Ceschia et al. (2023), working from full tables of bounds, name Abdullah and Turabieh (2012) and Kiefer et al. (2017) as holding almost all best results within the ITC timeout. Chen et al. drew their judgement from a 35-paper sample, so this review follows Ceschia et al. for state-of-the-art claims and uses the tables in Ceschia et al. (2026) as reference values. Abdipoor et al. (2023) give no per-instance numbers at all, so they cannot serve this purpose.

ITC-2019 is the richer alternative. Its 30 instances come from ten institutions through UniTime and combine time and room assignment with student sectioning, five-minute time granularity, multi-week patterns and travel times; an online validator exists, and 25 of the 30 instances still lack proven optima (Müller et al., 2025). Bashab et al. (2023, p. 6472) claim that "University course timetabling faces an absence of benchmark data", a statement already out of date when the paper was submitted in 2022.

Choosing Track 3 as the primary benchmark is still defensible. Its structure (courses, curricula, teachers, rooms with capacity, periods as day-slot pairs) is close to AITU's model, its constraints are simple to encode, and its published averages, optima and lower bounds allow direct comparison. Its gaps relative to AITU should be stated as threats to validity. It has no student sectioning, so the stream-enrollment module is untested by it. Room capacity is soft, rooms have no features, and lectures occupy a single period, so laboratory blocks are not represented. All instances come from Italian universities; the ITC-2019 institutions (Masaryk, Purdue, AGH, Maryville, Yachay Tech, Turkish-German University, İstanbul Kültür, LUMS, Bethlehem, Nairobi) do not include a Central Asian university either. Most important for this design, feasibility on comp is trivial. Ceschia et al. (2026, p. 3) report that their random construction "almost always terminates with a complete solution, indicating that it is easy to construct assignments that satisfy all hard constraints besides Conflicts", and that hill climbing removes the remaining conflicts in milliseconds. A Track 3 evaluation will therefore exercise the simulated annealing phase and say little about backtracking with MRV, LCV and forward checking, or about the explanation module. Tightened or deliberately infeasible variants of comp (fewer rooms, extra teacher unavailabilities), synthetic AITU-like data at high room load and, if time allows, the smallest ITC-2019 instances such as yach-fal17 (417 classes) can cover those parts. Ceschia et al. (2023) also advise keeping tuning instances separate from benchmark instances to avoid over-tuning.

### 2.3 Exact and heuristic solution methods

Two claims in the literature appear to contradict each other. Chen et al. (2021, p. 106516) write that "Exact algorithms are guaranteed to provide optimal solutions but they are only applicable to small sized problems". Yet a fix-and-optimize MIP matheuristic won ITC-2019 and another MIP took second place (Ceschia et al., 2023; Müller et al., 2025), and exact methods have proven optimality on most Track 3 instances. The computing budget explains the difference. The ITC-2019 winner needed up to 756 GB of memory, and its full model of the largest instance did not fit into 256 GB (Müller et al., 2025). Rappos et al. (2022) needed 262.2 hours over 1,845 runs only to make pu-proj-fal19 feasible, ended with gaps of up to 378% to the best known solutions, and concluded that "Our approach worked well for small and medium problems but poorly when the number of student was large" (p. 400). Ceschia et al. (2026, p. 2) note that the comp optima came "often at the cost of several hours of computation". Exact methods are strong for bounds and offline proofs; under an interactive budget of seconds or minutes, they need decomposition or a heuristic around them. Ceschia et al. (2023, p. 16) conclude that "both exact and (meta)heuristic techniques have their role and their chance to emerge, depending on the specific formulation and the competition ground."

Smaller exact models remain practical. Harrabi et al. (2024) present an ILP-based decision support system at the High Institute of Business Studies of Carthage that maximises teacher preferences and, according to the abstract, improved computation time and solution quality over the manual process. Since only the abstract could be read, its model size, solver and runtimes are unknown, and it cannot be compared with anything quantitatively. Antunes-Batista et al. (2026) found that a Gurobi ILP for room allocation was the most reliable on hard constraints but "limited to a single non-diverse solution" (p. 669).

OR-Tools CP-SAT is the exact baseline chosen for AITU. Its developers describe it as an integer-only solver built on a SAT engine with lazy clause generation, extended with a simplex, cuts and a portfolio of search workers that share information, and they claim it is competitive with leading MIP solvers on purely integer problems (Perron et al., 2023). That source is a two-page invited-talk abstract written by the developers, so it supports the description of the architecture more than the performance claims. Independent support comes from the MiniZinc Challenge 2025, where CP-SAT won gold in the Fixed, Free and Parallel categories and its local-search variant won the Local Search category (MiniZinc, 2025). None of the reviewed timetabling papers reports CP-SAT results on ITC-2007 or ITC-2019: Ceschia et al. (2026) list MIP, SAT/MaxSAT and answer set programming among exact approaches, and no ITC-2019 entrant used CP-SAT (Müller et al., 2025). AITU's baseline therefore cannot be checked against published CP-SAT numbers and must be compared with published bounds instead. The pairwise "forbidden combination" constraints of Rappos et al. (2022) and Steiner et al. (2025), written as x + y + x + y ≤ 3, translate directly into Boolean clauses.

On the heuristic side, simulated annealing has the strongest record among single-solution methods. Kirkpatrick et al. (1983) introduced it as an analogy with annealing in solids: worse moves are accepted with a Metropolis probability, and the temperature is lowered by slow stages until the system freezes, with the schedule determined empirically. Chen et al. (2021) found that five of the seven single-solution approaches in their benchmark sample were based on SA, and Abdipoor et al. (2023, pp. 10-11) conclude that "Simulated annealing has shown remarkable capabilities in solving UCTP benchmark datasets ... and appears to be the best-performing single solution-based method", while population methods did worse in the competitions because of their time cost. Bashab et al. (2023) count only 7 of 128 papers using SA against 20 using genetic algorithms. That count reflects how often a method is published and so does not weaken the performance evidence for SA.

Ceschia et al. (2026) provide the most detailed recent SA design for Track 3. Their method starts from a three-stage construction, then runs SA with a "LectureKick" neighbourhood whose nine preconditions keep every move hard-feasible, delta evaluation over auxiliary data structures, geometric cooling with a cut-off on accepted moves, and iteration-based stopping for machine independence. It beat every earlier local search method with statistical significance and was level with the overall state of the art (average rank 1.79 against 2.00 and 2.21, "not statistically significant"). Its weaknesses are the ones the review literature predicts. Tuning took 30,000 irace experiments per stage on 996 generated instances, and dedicated tuning on one instance family (Erlangen) cut cost by about 14.5%, which the authors read as showing that their approach "is rather sensitive to parameter values" (p. 11). Abdipoor et al. (2023, p. 10) call parameter setting "a major drawback of meta-heuristics". AITU cannot repeat an irace campaign each semester, so its SA should start from published parameter ranges and include a small tuning experiment in the evaluation.

Constraint-based local search has a separate lineage. Müller's (2005) doctoral thesis introduced iterative forward search, which works over partial assignments, together with conflict-based statistics. His ITC-2007 solver, built on these ideas, was a finalist in all three tracks and won two (Müller, 2009); Ceschia et al. (2023) and Abdipoor et al. (2023) both record it as the Track 3 winner. The same family now powers UniTime. Its solver runs on one core with 4 GB in two hours on the ITC-2019 instances, and the organisers rate it as the second-best solver and better than the matheuristics on the largest instances (Müller et al., 2025); since the organisers develop UniTime, this assessment is not independent. Population-based multiobjective methods sit at the other extreme. Antunes-Batista et al. (2026) compared NSGA-II, NSGA-III and MOEA/D on one real room-allocation instance of about 10,000 students and found MOEA/D best (normalised hypervolume 0.998-1.000), but each experiment ran 13.5 to 16.7 hours, the data are not public, and the text contradicts itself about which configuration used a constructive start.

These results support splitting the engine into a feasibility phase and an optimisation phase. Müller et al. (2025, p. 262) state that "The metaheuristic methods are naturally stronger in approaching the optimization than the satisfaction component", and the SA entry in ITC-2019 left eight instances unsolved within 24 hours. Rappos et al. (2022) also separate a feasibility stage from an improvement stage, Ceschia et al. (2026) run SA only in the feasible space, and Chen et al. (2021) and Bashab et al. (2023) both classify such designs as "two-stage", in which "the satisfaction of the soft constraints is only conducted when achieving the feasible timetable" (Bashab et al., 2023, p. 6469). Abdipoor et al. (2023) describe two-stage hybrids such as PARTIALCOL with SA (Lewis & Thompson, 2015, as cited in Abdipoor et al., 2023) and TSSP with SA and reheating (Goh et al., as cited in Abdipoor et al., 2023); the latter is state of the art on the Socha, ITC-2002 and ITC-2007 Track 2 benchmarks. AITU's plan (backtracking with MRV, LCV and forward checking to find a first feasible timetable, then SA over feasibility-preserving moves) follows this pattern. Chen et al. (2021) list the saturation-degree heuristic, which schedules next the event with the fewest remaining suitable periods, among the graph-colouring heuristics used in timetabling; it is the same idea as MRV. The two-stage design has a cost that the thesis should acknowledge: if SA may only move between feasible timetables, the feasible space can be poorly connected under tight resources, a problem Lewis and Thompson studied (as cited in Abdipoor et al., 2023). Kick moves of the kind Ceschia et al. (2026) use, which relocate a displaced lecture, are one remedy.

### 2.4 Explaining infeasibility

Steiner et al. (2025, p. 284) write of their Graz case that "it can hardly be expected that a feasible solution with a perfect timetable for all courses and all students can be computed, but infeasibility will be the default outcome." If infeasibility is normal, a timetabling system has to say why it happened. The standard tool is QuickXplain (Junker, 2004). Given a preference order over constraints, it finds a preferred conflict, that is, a conflict that is minimal in the sense that "each proper subset of C has a solution", by divide and conquer, using at most 2k·log(n/k) + 2k consistency checks for a conflict of size k among n constraints (Junker, 2004, Table 4, p. 170). Junker's example is that a problem with one million constraints and a conflict of eight elements needs between 33 and 270 checks. The method works with any CP, SAT or description-logic solver and underlay the explanation facility of an industrial configuration tool. These properties suit AITU, whose own backtracking solver can serve as the consistency check.

Gamba et al. (2023) show the limit of subset-minimality. They point out that "MUS extraction algorithms do not guarantee subset minimality or optimality with respect to a given cost function" (p. 709) and found empirically that MUS-based explanations often missed much cheaper ones. Their OCUS algorithm finds cost-optimal unsatisfiable subsets through an implicit hitting-set loop between a MIP and a SAT solver; their best configuration (OCUS-Split with incrementality) produced a first explanation in 2.58 s and further steps in 0.45 s on average, though its worst single step took 18.05 s. Other configurations were far slower in the worst case: 523.67 s for plain OCUS and 3,452.20 s for OCUS with incrementality but without splitting. The evidence comes entirely from puzzles, and the cost weights (60, 100 and 1) were chosen without users. Gamba et al. explain the solution of satisfiable puzzles step by step. Applying their machinery to an infeasible timetable is an inference from the MUS-MCS duality they use, and no reviewed paper has tested it on timetabling.

Steiner et al. (2025) provide the only timetabling case in the set that handles infeasibility explicitly. They computed an irreducible infeasible subsystem of their ILP, found the conflicts in the section-slot and capacity constraints, then added elastic variables with tiered penalties (extra sections, and overcapacity of up to 5 or up to 20 seats at different costs) so that the model shows where repairs are needed and how expensive they are. From this they derive five practical actions: add sections, increase capacity, optimise only the main subjects, drop rare subject pairs, or lower the workload for which a conflict-free timetable is guaranteed. The authors argue that this "elastic" approach "provides valuable insight for the decision maker into which of the actions mentioned above is appropriate and effective" (p. 311), but the explanation is aimed at planners working on the model, and the paper reports no evaluation with them. Other sources handle infeasibility less transparently. Rappos et al. (2022) measure it through slack variables that indicate how infeasible a run is without saying why, and Antunes-Batista et al. (2026, p. 667) add "a heavily penalized dummy room" so that the model always returns an answer.

Vasileiou and Yeoh (2025) address the user end. TRACE-CS encodes degree-planning constraints in SAT, computes subset-minimal explanations for contrastive questions with MUS and MCS machinery, and uses GPT-4.1 only to parse the question and to rephrase constraint labels. On 550 queries it was correct in 100% of cases against 54.1% for GPT-4.1 alone, with shorter answers (81.8 against 159.4 words) but slower responses (14.7 s against 4.3 s). The language model tended to "generate non-minimal explanations that included most or all applicable constraints" even when asked for minimal ones (p. 656). The 100% figure follows largely from the soundness of the symbolic explainer, the authors graded correctness themselves, the domain is assignment of courses to semesters, which is much smaller than weekly timetabling, and there was no user study. The paper does support a clear division of labour: the solver produces the explanation, and language generation, whether from templates or a model, only verbalises it.

Existing timetabling engines use simpler tools. The UniTime solver manual offers lists of unassigned classes, reasons such as "no available placement", per-class suggestions and conflict-based statistics that trace conflicts to competing classes, and it advises users to contact administrators when the cause is unclear (UniTime, n.d.). FET's documentation advises users who get an impossible timetable to remove constraints until a solution appears (FET, n.d.). Neither documents a plain-language report of a minimal set of conflicting constraints.

For AITU, these sources point to the following design. Constraints should be grouped and labelled at the level a scheduler in the Registrar's Office understands ("teacher X is available only on Monday and Tuesday", "room 301 seats 30"), and QuickXplain should run over these groups in a preference order that puts specific, changeable constraints before structural rules. Junker's preferred conflicts are the cheap counterpart of Gamba's cost-optimal ones. Each conflict should be rendered from templates in Kazakh, Russian and English, with repair hints in the spirit of Steiner's elastic variables. The computation should run asynchronously with a timeout, and the thesis should report time to first explanation, as Gamba et al. do. None of the sources measures whether such explanations help real users, so the project can at most report a small usability check.

### 2.5 Stream enrollment and course allocation

Enrollment in course streams is studied in two literatures that rarely cite each other. In timetabling, student sectioning is the novelty of ITC-2019 (Müller et al., 2025, p. 247: "The key novelty lies in the combination of student sectioning with standard time and room assignment"). Chen et al. (2021) list sectioning with unequal section sizes as an open research issue, and Abdipoor et al. (2023) note that it increases complexity. Steiner et al. (2025) separate the steps: they build the timetable first and then match more than 2,000 real students to it, with a minimum-load bound τ for fairness and a round-robin distribution of leftover seats that favours students close to graduation. Reviewing Müller and Murray (2010), they describe a spectrum of initial, batch and online sectioning (Steiner et al., 2025, p. 281). AITU sits at the online end: the timetable is fixed and students enroll in real time.

Steiner et al. contain two internal inconsistencies that should not be repeated. The paper says on p. 279 that more than 40% of courses are passed later than the prescribed term, and on p. 301 that over 60% are taken late. It also reports a 0.33% optimality gap for the base case after one hour (p. 302) and then calls the same case solvable "to optimality in a surprisingly short running time" (p. 305).

Market design supplies the formal model and the fairness criteria. Soumalias et al. (2024) define course allocation with course capacities q_j and, for each student, a set of permissible schedules Ψ_i that "encapsulates both scheduling constraints as well as any student-specific constraints (e.g., prerequisites)" (p. 7). AITU stream enrollment is a restricted instance of this model: each stream is a "course" with a seat capacity, and clash-freedom is part of Ψ_i. Their machine-learning extension of Course Match raised average and minimum student utility in simulation, by about 7-11% and 17-29% respectively, by asking students about ten pairwise questions. The evidence is simulation only, calibrated to one laboratory experiment, and the full paper is an arXiv preprint whose only peer-reviewed form is an abstract at EC '24. It is useful here for the model and for the idea that fairness is measured by the minimum utility as well as the average.

Romero-Medina and Triossi (2024) criticise the first-come-first-served registration that the AITU Smart Schedule MVP uses. They note that "Traditional mechanisms, such as the first-come-first-served mechanism, are commonly used but face criticism for inducing a competitive race that can overload systems and lead to inequitable outcomes. Also, the mechanism does not respect students' priorities" (p. 3). Their conditional acceptance mechanism implements stable allocations, with no justified envy and no wasted seats, in Nash equilibrium, and its repeated version covers add rounds on the condition that students cannot drop courses (p. 12). They also observe that slot-specific preferences fit "when mandatory courses are offered in simultaneous sections" (p. 2), which is the AITU stream situation. The paper is purely theoretical and assumes students who know each other's preferences and best-respond. When streams of different courses clash in time, a student's choices become interdependent, which the paper does not discuss.

Correctness and fairness are separate properties and need separate evidence. Correctness, meaning that no stream is overfilled and no room is double-booked, can be guaranteed by the database. PostgreSQL exclusion constraints "ensure that if any two rows are compared on the specified columns or expressions using the specified operators, at least one of these operator comparisons will return false or null", and the documented pattern `EXCLUDE USING GIST (room WITH =, during WITH &&)` with `btree_gist` rejects overlapping bookings of the same room (PostgreSQL Global Development Group, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). The seat counter relies on a conditional update, whose safety under concurrent transactions depends on how PostgreSQL's Read Committed level re-evaluates the condition. That behaviour is not yet verified against the documentation, so the thesis should cite the transaction isolation chapter directly and back it with the concurrency tests planned in the technical specification: 200 concurrent requests against a 30-seat stream, and 0 overflows for 500 concurrent requests to one stream. Fairness cannot be guaranteed this way. First-come-first-served with database guarantees removes the technical race that causes overbooking, but the economic race described by Romero-Medina and Triossi remains: whoever clicks first gets the preferred teacher and time. The thesis should say so openly. A defensible extension is a short window in which students rank streams, a batch allocation by priority, and then an add-only first-come-first-served round, which the no-drop result supports.

### 2.6 Timetabling information systems

The applied system papers in the set are the weakest sources. Roy et al. (2022) describe a Laravel and MySQL "Smart Class Routine Management System" with administrator, teacher and student modules. The introduction says "we have built an intelligent routine management system using the genetic algorithm" (p. 38), but no algorithm, constraint model, data or evaluation appears, the routine is created by an administrator, and the claim that there are "no works reported in the literature about the implementation of a complete dynamic class routine management system" (p. 39) ignores UniTime and decades of related work. The journal's own indexing list does not include Scopus or Web of Science. Padshetty et al. (2025) describe a browser application that stores data in localStorage and validates manually entered slots against four rules. Its title promises automatic generation, its conclusion claims optimal allocation, and its literature section addresses the reader with "For your project" (p. 364), while its first reference garbles the authors, title and journal of a real 2025 review. Both papers are useful here only as negative examples. Their role-based views, configurable break slots and teacher change requests subject to approval are reasonable requirements, but neither paper is evidence for any algorithmic or usability claim.

The UniTime line of work shows what a stronger system record looks like: the algorithms are published (Müller, 2005, 2009), benchmarked in competitions and documented in a user manual. Even there, the published evaluation is algorithmic. None of the sixteen recent papers reports a user study of a timetabling system. Steiner et al. (2025) planned deployment at Graz for 2024/25 but report no outcome, MLCM has not been fielded (Soumalias et al., 2024), and TRACE-CS was not tested with users (Vasileiou & Yeoh, 2025). The gap between research and practice is stated repeatedly. Chen et al. (2021, p. 106522) note that "state-of-the-art approaches in benchmark UCTTP are not fully utilised in real-world UCTTP"; Abdipoor et al. (2023, p. 14) say the gap "is still large, which has made many universities rely on manual timetabling"; Antunes-Batista et al. (2026, p. 663) report that room allocation "is still predominantly performed manually due to the inefficiency of existing solutions"; and Bashab et al. (2023) call for more timetabling software and tools. Ceschia et al. (2023) leave system and interface questions out of scope by design. No source in the set covers interactive room booking on a floor map, so that part of AITU rests on engineering practice and the PostgreSQL documentation and has no research literature behind it.

### 2.7 Research gap and design implications

Taken together, the sources show strong but separate research streams. Timetabling optimisation has standard formulations, validators and solvers that reach proven optima on Track 3 (Ceschia et al., 2023, 2026). Explanation research has sound algorithms for minimal and cost-optimal conflicts (Gamba et al., 2023; Junker, 2004) and one hybrid system for course planning (Vasileiou & Yeoh, 2025). Course allocation has formal models and fairness criteria (Romero-Medina & Triossi, 2024; Soumalias et al., 2024). In the reviewed set, no system generates a weekly university timetable, evaluates its engine on a public benchmark, explains infeasibility in plain language to the scheduler, and supports concurrent stream enrollment and room booking with guaranteed correctness. The existing engines offer expert diagnostics at most (UniTime, n.d.; FET, n.d.), and the applied web systems offer neither optimisation nor evaluation (Padshetty et al., 2025; Roy et al., 2022). Three narrower gaps sit inside this one. Explanation methods have been tested on puzzles and degree plans, never on weekly timetables. Published CP-SAT results on the ITC benchmarks are absent from the reviewed sources. No source evaluates generated timetables or explanations with their users, and none comes from a Central Asian university.

AITU Smart Schedule addresses the integration gap and can add modest evidence on the narrower ones. Each design decision is listed below with the sources that support it.
